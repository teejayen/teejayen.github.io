There are three tiers of AI training in Australia right now. Two of them are well-served. The third -- the one that matters most -- is almost completely empty.

## Tier one: free awareness

The government and the big platforms are handling this tier. CommBank partnered with OpenAI to offer free AI training to Australian businesses. State governments run digital literacy programs. Vendors host webinars showing what their tools can do.

This is genuinely valuable. It gets AI onto the radar of business owners who might otherwise ignore it. It answers the baseline question: "What is this, and should I care?"

But awareness is no longer the bottleneck. [52% of Australian businesses](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/business/innovation-and-technology/ai-adoption-australian-business/latest-release) are already using AI in some form. Most people have heard of ChatGPT. Most have tried it at least once.

The problem is what comes after awareness. You know AI exists. You've typed something into ChatGPT and got a vaguely useful response. Now what?

The free programs don't answer that. They can't -- they're built for scale, not specificity. They tell you AI is useful. They don't tell you which tool to use for your electrical quoting workflow, or how to structure a prompt that drafts an engagement letter your partner would actually sign off on, or how to turn a voice memo from a site visit into structured job notes.

## Tier three: corporate training

At the other end, there's enterprise training. The Big Four consulting firms, the Australian Institute of Management, specialised corporate educators. These programs run $440 to $2,000+ per day. They're built for organisations with dedicated learning and development budgets, 50+ employees, and implementation teams.

They're good at what they do. But they're not designed for the business owner with 12 staff who's wondering if AI can help them get quotes out the same day instead of three days later.

The content is too generic (built for enterprises, not SMBs). The price is too high (a day of training costs more than a month's AI subscription). The format is too theoretical (slide decks about "AI strategy" when what you need is someone showing you how to draft a customer email that doesn't sound like a robot).

## The gap in the middle

Between free awareness and $2,000 corporate programs, there's almost nothing.

No practical, vendor-neutral, industry-specific AI training for business owners in the $50-150 range. The kind of training where you walk in wondering if AI is relevant to your plumbing business, your accounting firm, your cafe, your physiotherapy practice, your real estate agency, your transport company, or your not-for-profit -- and you walk out with specific things to try on Monday morning.

This is the gap that matters most, because this is where most Australian businesses sit. The [ABS data](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/business/innovation-and-technology/ai-adoption-australian-business/latest-release) tells the story: 48% of businesses aren't using AI at all. Of those that are, most are stuck at the "occasionally ask ChatGPT a question" stage. A third of non-adopters say they don't know where to start.

They don't need more awareness. They need clarity about application.

## What practical training actually looks like

The difference between useful AI training and a waste of two hours comes down to a few things.

**It's vendor-neutral.** If the presenter is selling a specific tool, the training is a product demo dressed up as education. Useful AI training shows you principles that work across ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot -- whatever you end up using. The tool matters less than how you use it.

**It's industry-specific.** "AI can help with email" is a useless statement. "AI can draft a scope-of-works review that catches the five things most likely to cause a variation dispute" is useful. "AI can turn your voice memo from the site visit into structured job notes with a parts list" is useful. The specificity is the value.

**It includes practical frameworks.** Not "here's what AI can do" but "here's how to brief AI so it gives you something you'd actually use." The difference between getting rubbish from ChatGPT and getting genuinely useful output is almost always the quality of your input. That's teachable -- and it takes about 15 minutes to learn properly.

**It has a pathway beyond awareness.** You've seen the demos. You've got the frameworks. Now what? Good training gives you a clear "Monday morning" action list -- specific tasks to try, with the prompts to try them. Not inspiration. Implementation.

**It's honest about limitations.** AI is a tool, not magic. It's confidently wrong sometimes. It can't replace your 20 years of trade experience or your professional judgement. Any training that oversells AI's capabilities is setting people up for disappointment. The most useful thing a trainer can do is be honest about where AI genuinely helps and where it's a waste of time.

## Why the gap exists

The economics explain it. Free programs are funded by governments and platforms with awareness mandates. Corporate training is funded by organisations with training budgets. The middle -- practical SMB training -- has no natural funder.

Running a quality workshop for 30-50 people at $80-120 a head requires deep industry knowledge, live demo capability, and the ability to adapt on the fly to a room full of different businesses. That's a narrow skillset. Most AI trainers come from enterprise backgrounds and default to slides and theory. Most industry practitioners who understand the workflows haven't mapped them to AI tools yet.

The result: business owners with real AI questions and no one to answer them. Or worse, they attend a free webinar, get told "AI is amazing," try ChatGPT once, get a mediocre result, and conclude it's not for them.

That conclusion is wrong. They just didn't get the right introduction.

## What would actually help

Here's what I think practical AI training for SMBs needs to include:

**Live demonstrations, not slides.** Show a real business task being completed with AI in real time. Draft a quote. Summarise a contract. Respond to a negative Google review. Turn a voice memo into structured notes. When people see it happen live -- not in a screenshot, not in a case study, but right in front of them -- the penny drops.

**A prompting framework that actually works.** Most people type "write me an email" and get rubbish back. Then they conclude AI is useless. What they need is a structured approach to briefing AI -- the same way you'd brief a new employee. Give it the background, tell it what you want, explain why, show it an example, and set the boundaries. This is learnable in 15 minutes and it transforms the output immediately.

**A real business workshopped live.** Theory is forgettable. Watching a real business -- one from the audience, with real workflows and real pain points -- get analysed and mapped for AI opportunities is memorable. Every person in the room sees themselves in it.

**Industry-specific examples.** The plumber needs to see quoting and SWMS generation. The accountant needs to see document summarisation and engagement letter drafting. The cafe owner needs to see Google review responses and social content. The physio needs to see recall automation and patient handouts. Generic "AI for business" examples don't land. Industry-specific ones do.

**A Monday morning action list.** Not "go explore AI." Specific tasks: "Draft your next quote with AI using this prompt. Summarise that contract you've been putting off. Run your proposal through three review perspectives before you send it." Concrete, actionable, immediate.

## The awareness phase is over

For most businesses, the question is no longer "should I care about AI?" It's "where do I actually start with my specific business?"

That question deserves a better answer than a free webinar or a $2,000 corporate program. It deserves practical, industry-specific training that meets business owners where they are -- curious, time-poor, and ready to try something if someone just shows them what to try.

The gap is real. And it's exactly where the most value sits.
