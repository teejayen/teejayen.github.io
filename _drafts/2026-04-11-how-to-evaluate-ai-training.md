There's more AI training available now than at any point in history. Free government programs, vendor webinars, corporate workshops, online courses, YouTube tutorials, LinkedIn influencers, and a growing number of in-person events.

Some of it is genuinely useful. Some of it is a thinly disguised sales pitch. Some of it is someone who discovered ChatGPT three months ago repackaging basic tips as expertise.

If you're a business owner considering AI training -- for yourself or your team -- here are five questions to ask before you invest your time and money.

## 1. Is it vendor-neutral or selling a specific tool?

This is the first filter and it eliminates a lot of noise.

If the training is run by Microsoft, it will recommend Copilot. If it's run by Google, it will showcase Gemini. If it's run by a software vendor, it will conclude that their platform is the answer. That's not training -- it's a product demo with a longer introduction.

Vendor-specific training has its place. If you've already committed to Microsoft 365 Copilot and want to get more from it, a Microsoft-run session makes sense. But if you're still working out whether AI is relevant to your business and which tools to use, vendor-neutral training is what you need.

**The test:** Does the trainer recommend a specific tool regardless of your situation? Or do they explain principles that work across ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini, Copilot, and whatever comes next? The principles are more valuable than the tool, because the tools will change. The principles won't.

## 2. Does it cover your industry specifically?

"AI for business" is a useless category. The AI applications that matter for an electrical contractor are completely different from those that matter for an accounting firm, which are different again from a cafe, a physio practice, a real estate agency, a freight operator, or a community services organisation.

Generic training tells you "AI can help with emails." Industry-specific training tells you "AI can draft a scope-of-works review that catches the five clauses most likely to cause a variation dispute" or "AI can turn your site visit voice memo into structured job notes with a parts list before you reach the next job."

**The test:** Look at the examples in the marketing material. Are they generic (write an email, summarise a document) or specific to your industry? If the trainer can't give examples relevant to your work, they probably don't understand your work.

This doesn't mean the training needs to be exclusively for your industry. A cross-industry workshop can be excellent if the frameworks are universal and the examples span multiple sectors. But if every example is about writing LinkedIn posts and summarising meeting notes, it's not built for you.

## 3. Is there a practical component or just theory?

The gap between "AI can do this" and "I can do this with AI" is entirely practical. You cross it by doing, not by watching slides.

Good AI training includes live demonstrations -- real tasks completed with AI in real time, not screenshots of outputs. Better training includes hands-on practice where you try the techniques yourself during the session. Best training includes a live workshop where a real business scenario gets mapped and analysed.

**The test:** Does the agenda include live demonstrations, hands-on exercises, or interactive workshops? Or is it exclusively presentation slides and case studies? If you're never going to touch an AI tool during the training, you'll leave with inspiration but not capability.

The difference matters. You can watch someone ride a bike all day. You learn to ride by getting on one.

## 4. Is there a pathway to implementation or just awareness?

Awareness training tells you AI exists and it's useful. Implementation training tells you what to do on Monday morning.

The best AI training gives you a concrete action list: specific tasks to try in your business, with the prompts to try them, using tools you can access today. It connects the frameworks you learned to the actual workflows in your business.

**The test:** Will you leave the session with a list of specific things to try in your business this week? Or will you leave thinking "that was interesting" without a clear next step?

Good indicators: the training provides prompt templates you can copy and adapt, recommends specific tools with free tiers you can start with immediately, and includes follow-up resources or support.

Bad indicators: the training ends with "go explore AI" or "consider how AI could help your business." If the action item is to "consider," the training didn't go far enough.

## 5. What's the cost relative to the potential value?

AI training ranges from free to $2,000+ per day. Neither extreme is inherently better.

Free training (government programs, vendor webinars, CommBank x OpenAI) is excellent for awareness. If you're starting from zero -- "I've never used ChatGPT and want to understand what this is" -- free programs are the right starting point.

Corporate training ($440-$2,000/day from AIM, Big Four, specialised providers) is built for organisations with dedicated L&D budgets and implementation teams. The content is typically deeper, and the format often includes multi-day programs with ongoing support. If you're a 200-person company deploying AI across the organisation, this makes sense.

The middle tier ($50-150 per person) is the most underserved. This is where practical, SMB-focused training lives -- if you can find it. At this price point, you should expect industry-specific content, live demonstrations, practical frameworks, and a clear pathway to implementation.

**The test:** Calculate the value of the time AI could save you. If the training teaches you to save one hour per week, that's worth roughly $2,000-4,000 per year in recovered productive time (depending on how you value your hours). Even expensive training pays for itself quickly if the content is practical and you actually implement it.

The more important cost is your time. A half-day or evening session is a manageable commitment for a busy business owner. A three-day program is a significant investment that needs to deliver proportionally more value.

## Bonus: how to spot genuine expertise

The AI training market has a credibility problem. The barrier to entry is low -- anyone who's used ChatGPT for a month can call themselves an AI consultant. Here are a few signals that separate genuine expertise from surface-level knowledge.

**Do they understand your industry?** Ask a specific question about your workflows. Someone who's actually worked with businesses like yours will give a specific answer. Someone who hasn't will pivot to generic advice.

**Are they honest about limitations?** Genuine AI practitioners know where AI fails. They'll tell you not to use it for engineering calculations, that it hallucinates confidently, that the output always needs review. Anyone who presents AI as universally reliable either doesn't know or doesn't care.

**Do they use AI themselves?** Ask what they use it for in their own work. The best trainers are practitioners who use AI daily in real business operations -- not educators who've studied it from the outside.

**Can they show, not just tell?** Live demonstrations are the credibility test. Anyone can prepare slides with cherry-picked screenshots. Running a live demo -- where the AI might not produce the perfect output on the first try -- shows genuine comfort with the tools and the ability to iterate in real time.

**Do they have a framework, or just tips?** Tips are useful but forgettable. A structured framework -- a repeatable approach to getting good output from AI -- is what changes behaviour long-term. The training should give you a mental model, not just a list of tricks.

## The decision framework

If you're on the fence about AI training, run through this:

1. **Do I know where to start with AI in my specific business?** If no, you need training that provides clarity about application, not just awareness.
2. **Have I tried AI and been disappointed?** If yes, the issue is likely input quality, not AI capability. Training that focuses on frameworks (how to brief AI properly) will fix this.
3. **Do I have time for a multi-day program?** If no, look for focused sessions (2-3 hours) that prioritise practical frameworks and leave you with an action list.
4. **Is the training vendor-neutral and industry-relevant?** If both, it's likely worth your time. If neither, keep looking.
5. **Will I actually implement what I learn?** The most expensive training in the world is worthless if you don't try anything afterwards. The best training makes implementation easy by giving you specific prompts, tools, and tasks to try immediately.

AI training is worth your time when it moves you from "AI is interesting" to "I know exactly what to try in my business on Monday morning." If the training you're considering doesn't promise that -- or can't deliver it -- save your money and your afternoon.
