I've been thinking about farming.

Not the kind where you extract nutrients until the soil dies, then move on. The other kind - syntropic agroforestry - where each cycle leaves the system richer than before. More biomass. More biodiversity. More resilience.

My backyard runs on these principles. Bananas, berries, fruit trees - always something producing, always something decomposing back into the soil. I once helped establish 600 metres of tree row at a macadamia farm using the same approach. You plant density and diversity, let succession do the work, and the system gets richer over time.

It's the opposite of entropy. Where entropy degrades, syntropy builds.

And I keep seeing the same pattern in how we're building AI systems.

---

Most AI usage is extractive. You prompt, you get output, the conversation dies. Next session starts from zero. Every interaction costs tokens but accumulates nothing.

It's the monoculture approach. High yield, no compounding.

When I built [Arc](/building-arc-a-thinking-partner-that-remembers/), I wasn't trying to be clever. I was trying to stop losing context. But looking back, what I actually built was a syntropic system - one where each interaction deposits something back.

The journal grows richer. Patterns emerge across entries. The system remembers what I'm tracking, what I'm building, where I've been stuck. Each session feeds the next.

---

Ernst Götsch, who developed syntropic agriculture, has this principle: every intervention should leave the system more complex, more fertile, more alive.

Applied to AI tools:

**Entropic AI systems** reset every conversation. They optimise for immediate output. They treat you as a new user every time. Context is a cost to be minimised.

**Syntropic AI systems** accumulate context. Each interaction adds to a substrate that future interactions can draw from. The system gets more useful the longer you use it - not because the model improved, but because the context layer deepened.

This is more than memory. It's about structure.

I'm borrowing the term "syntropic" here. In agroforestry, it means something precise - succession toward complexity, biomass accumulation, life building on life. An AI context layer isn't literally syntropic. But the pattern rhymes: systems that get richer with use rather than depleting with each extraction.

---

But here's where I have to be honest. This frame has a hole in it.

I'm writing about syntropic patterns while using infrastructure that is almost certainly extractive at a planetary level.

The numbers aren't comforting. A single ChatGPT query produces [roughly 2-5 grams of CO2](https://www.sustainabilitybynumbers.com/p/carbon-footprint-chatgpt). That sounds small until you scale it. By 2026, AI systems alone could have a carbon footprint [equivalent to New York City](https://www.euronews.com/next/2025/12/20/ai-data-centres-could-have-a-carbon-footprint-that-matches-small-european-country-new-stud) - somewhere between 32 and 80 million tons of CO2. Water usage is projected at [312-764 billion litres](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2666389925002788), roughly equivalent to global bottled water consumption.

A [Cornell study in Nature Sustainability](https://news.cornell.edu/stories/2025/11/roadmap-shows-environmental-impact-ai-data-center-boom) found that by 2030, current AI growth rates would add 24-44 million metric tons of CO2 annually - the equivalent of putting 5-10 million additional cars on US roads.

And here's the uncomfortable part: [no AI company reports AI-specific environmental metrics](https://news.mit.edu/2025/explained-generative-ai-environmental-impact-0117). Anthropic, whose model I'm using right now, publishes thoughtfully on governance and safety risks but provides almost nothing on climate impact.

---

Is there global agreement on this? No.

The [EU AI Act](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/) mentions environmental protection but only encourages voluntary codes of conduct. The [OECD AI Principles](https://jsis.washington.edu/news/global-approaches-to-artificial-intelligence-regulation/) call for collaboration but have no enforcement. [Less than 1% of organisations](https://www.weforum.org/stories/2025/10/un-new-ai-governance-bodies/) have fully operationalised "responsible AI" in any comprehensive way.

The governance landscape is fragmented and years behind the technology. There's no consensus. No one's measuring. And usage keeps scaling.

---

I'm aware of the irony in BP's ["personal carbon footprint" campaign](https://grist.org/energy/footprint-fantasy/) - invented by their ad agency in 2004 to shift responsibility from corporations to individuals. [100 companies are responsible for 71% of global emissions](https://www.yesmagazine.org/environment/2022/01/31/climate-change-fossil-fuel-industry-individual-responsibility). Individual action is, as one researcher put it, "a micro truth in a macro lie."

But I don't think this lets me off the hook. [It's a false dichotomy](https://www.sustainabilitybynumbers.com/p/the-false-dichotomy-of-systemic-and). Individuals are part of the system - not big enough to move things alone, but capable of slowing things down if unwilling to budge.

The question isn't whether my AI usage "nets out." It probably doesn't. The question is what I do with the leverage I have.

---

My background is systems thinking - the discipline of seeing how parts interact, where feedback loops live, what makes complex systems stable or fragile.

Donella Meadows identified [twelve leverage points](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/) for intervening in complex systems, ranked from weakest to strongest:

At the bottom: parameters, numbers, taxes, subsidies. Easy to change but low impact.

At the top: the goals of the system. The paradigms - the shared assumptions underneath everything. And beyond that: the ability to transcend paradigms entirely.

Individual consumption choices operate at the lowest leverage points. Paradigm shifts - changing what people believe is possible or desirable - operate at the highest.

Where do I have leverage?

---

The [80,000 Hours research](https://80000hours.org/skills/software-tech/) on career impact suggests that for technologists, direct work on pressing problems beats almost everything else. AI safety. Pandemic prevention. Building tools for organisations tackling the hardest challenges.

Not earning to give. Not optimising consumption. Direct contribution.

This reframes the question. It's not "is my AI usage syntropic?" It's "what am I building with it?"

I founded [Redlands Garden Share](https://www.redlandsgardengroup.au/garden-share) a few years back. We meet monthly to swap excess produce, seeds, plants, knowledge. We've run permablitzes - showing up to transform neglected backyards into productive systems. It's small-scale, local, and entirely about spreading the pattern.

Is that "direct work on pressing problems"? I'm not sure. It's not AI safety or pandemic prevention. But it's paradigm work - demonstrating that another way of growing food is possible, and building the community infrastructure to support it.

The question of where else I could contribute directly - where my time and skills would have the most leverage on problems that matter - is one I'm still sitting with.

---

Research on [complex adaptive systems](https://climate.sustainability-directory.com/question/how-does-local-action-affect-global-climate/) suggests local actions aren't isolated. They create feedback loops. They trigger emergent properties at larger scales. The [UNDP's 30-year study](https://www.undp.org/publications/local-action-global-impact-celebrating-three-decades-local-action-people-and-planet) of small grants found that local community action on environmental issues creates systemic ripple effects.

This is the Meadows insight applied: paradigm shifts require people living the alternative. A syntropic farm isn't just a farm. It's evidence that another pattern works.

---

So where does this leave me?

I use extractive infrastructure to do potentially generative work. I can't calculate my way out of that contradiction. The numbers are uncertain, the accounting is impossible, and no one's keeping score anyway.

But the frame still matters.

Syntropic farming taught me that the goal isn't yield - it's building soil.

Systems thinking taught me to look for stocks, flows, and leverage points.

AI is teaching me that the model is less important than what surrounds it - and that the infrastructure underneath has costs I can't see.

Put them together: the most powerful contribution probably isn't optimising my footprint. It's finding where I can do direct work on systems that compound - and I'm still figuring out where that is.

In the meantime: build soil. Build community. Spread the pattern. And be honest about the contradictions along the way.
