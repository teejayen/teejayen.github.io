I'm not going to tell you AI won't replace your job. That would be dishonest. Some jobs will be displaced. Some already have been.

I'm also not going to tell you the robots are coming and we're all doomed. That's equally dishonest, and far less useful.

What I think is actually happening is more interesting than either of those stories. And the best way I can explain it is to tell you what it's done to my own work.

## What happened to my job

I've been in IT for 18 years. Systems administration, infrastructure, cloud migrations, security. I've never been a developer. I can read code, I write PowerShell competently, and I've spent nearly two decades solving problems with technology.

Twelve months ago, my work looked like this: I'd identify a problem, research a solution, manually configure systems, write documentation, and move to the next thing. The bottleneck was always execution. I could see what needed doing faster than I could do it.

Today, my work looks fundamentally different. When I hit a problem that would have taken me an afternoon of scripting -- pulling data from an API, restructuring a dataset, automating a repetitive process -- I describe what I need and AI writes the code. Not approximately. Precisely. In seconds.

A few weeks ago I needed to batch-process 12,000 screenshots through a vision API to extract searchable content. Previously: not feasible. I don't have the development skills to build that from scratch, and hiring someone to build it would cost thousands and take weeks. With AI: I described what I wanted, directed the approach, tested the output, iterated on the problems. Working tool, 30 minutes. [I wrote about it here](/2026/01/08/ship-tomorrow/).

This isn't a party trick. This is how I work now. Every day.

The shift is hard to describe to someone who hasn't experienced it. It's like the difference between typing a letter and dictating to someone who types faster than you can think. Except the "someone" also reads the documentation you haven't read yet, remembers the syntax you've forgotten, and suggests approaches you hadn't considered.

**AI drives the computer faster than I can.** I still guide it -- significantly. The judgement, the direction, the "is this actually what we need?" evaluation -- that's all mine. But the execution? The mechanical act of turning intent into working code, working documents, working systems? AI does that at a speed I can't match manually.

It's quicker for me to get AI to write a script to do something than it is for me to do it by hand. And I'm someone who's been doing this stuff for 18 years.

That's a level up. A genuine, permanent expansion of what I'm capable of producing in a day. And it's the most engaging work I've ever done -- because I'm not spending my time on the mechanical execution anymore. I'm spending it on the interesting part: figuring out what to build and why.

## The honest picture

My experience isn't universal. I have 18 years of technical background that gives me a head start in evaluating AI's output and directing its work. Not everyone has that.

But the pattern holds beyond the tech world.

Junior developer employment in the US [declined nearly 20%](https://stackoverflow.blog/2025/12/26/ai-vs-gen-z) from its peak in late 2022 to mid-2025. Not because AI replaced developers -- but because AI handles the grunt work that used to be the entry point. The career ladder didn't disappear. The bottom rungs got shorter.

That's real. Pretending otherwise doesn't help anyone.

But here's what the displacement narrative misses: **work isn't a fixed quantity that AI either does or doesn't do.** Work is a constantly evolving set of problems, and every time a tool handles one layer, new layers emerge.

When spreadsheets arrived, bookkeepers didn't vanish. The work changed. Manual ledger entry disappeared. Financial analysis expanded. The people who adapted thrived. The ones who insisted on doing things the old way struggled.

The pattern is consistent: automation eliminates tasks, not roles. The roles evolve. And the people who evolve with them end up doing more interesting, more valuable work.

That's what happened to me. AI didn't make my job smaller. It made it bigger. I can build things I couldn't build before. I can solve problems that were previously out of reach -- not because I was too slow, but because the execution cost was too high for one person.

## What's actually changing

The shift isn't "AI replaces humans." It's "AI changes the ratio of what humans spend their time on."

A senior accountant spends a significant chunk of their day on activities that AI can accelerate: reading documents, drafting correspondence, processing information, creating reports. The rest is the work only they can do: advising clients, exercising professional judgement, managing relationships, navigating ambiguity.

AI doesn't touch the judgement work. It compresses everything else.

The result isn't fewer accountants. It's accountants who can serve more clients, go deeper on advice, and spend less time on the administrative work that burns them out. The shape of the job changes. The value of the human in the loop goes up, not down.

I see this in my own work daily. I haven't become less of an IT professional. I've become one who can do in an afternoon what used to take a week. The skills I built over 18 years -- understanding systems, evaluating risk, knowing when something is wrong -- are more valuable now, not less. Because they're being applied at a scale I couldn't reach before.

The same pattern holds across industries:

**A plumber** finishes a site visit. Sitting in the van, he records a 45-second voice memo -- everything he noticed, parts to order, follow-up items. AI structures it into job notes, generates the quote, and the customer has it in their inbox before he reaches the next job. His competitor is still planning to "write it up tonight." The plumber's trade skill didn't change. His ability to act on it immediately did.

**A physiotherapist** sees her last patient at 5pm. By 5:15, referral letters for three patients are drafted, the recall list has been processed, and a new patient handout on ACL rehab has been created from her clinical notes. She used to do this paperwork from 6 to 8pm. Now she's home for dinner. The clinical judgement is still hers. The paperwork isn't.

**A not-for-profit director** has a $50,000 grant deadline in two weeks. Previously, that meant three days of writing from scratch -- pulling data from spreadsheets, rewriting the organisation's background section for the hundredth time, matching language to the funder's priorities. Now: AI drafts from the program data and last year's acquittal report. The director spends half a day reviewing, adding nuance, inserting current context. They submit three applications this quarter instead of one, because the admin didn't consume the time the mission needed.

In every case, AI handles the repetitive cognitive load. The human does the work that requires judgement, relationships, and experience. The work that's actually interesting.

## The part that's genuinely uncomfortable

I want to be honest about something: the transition isn't painless, and I'm wary of anyone who says it is.

The 58-year-old bookkeeper who's been preparing BAS returns the same way for the last 25 years is watching that skill become less valuable in real time. The knowledge isn't worthless -- someone still needs to understand the tax rules, the edge cases, the client context. But the mechanical act of data entry and form preparation? That's exactly the kind of repetitive cognitive work AI handles well.

That's not abstract for the person living it. That's their professional identity. That's the thing they've been good at -- the thing that's paid the mortgage and defined their working life. Watching it get compressed by a tool that didn't exist three years ago isn't exciting. It's disorienting.

And the "just learn to use AI" advice, while technically correct, glosses over the reality that learning a new way of working at 58 is harder than learning at 28. Not impossible -- but harder. The time pressure is different. The career runway is different. The psychological cost of admitting "the way I've been doing this isn't the best way anymore" is real.

I've felt a version of this myself, on a smaller scale. There's a strange moment when you realise AI can do something you spent years learning to do -- and it can do it faster. Your first reaction isn't excitement. It's something closer to "what was all that effort for?"

The answer, I've found, is that the effort gave you the judgement to direct the tool. The 18 years of IT experience are what let me evaluate whether AI's code is safe, whether its approach is sound, whether the output is actually solving the problem or just looking like it does. Without that experience, I'd be accepting bad output without knowing it.

The skill didn't become worthless. It became the foundation for a more powerful version of the skill. But getting there requires going through the disorientation first, and nobody should pretend that's trivial.

## The companies-of-one possibility

Here's where it gets genuinely exciting.

The [share of solo-founded US startups](https://www.siliconindia.com/news/startups/how-ai-tools-are-letting-solo-founders-build-empires-in-2026-nid-238909-cid-19.html) rose from 22% in 2015 to 38% in 2024. That was before the current wave of AI tools hit full stride.

There's a prediction floating around that [AI could enable a single person to run a billion-dollar company](https://www.nucamp.co/blog/solo-ai-tech-entrepreneur-2025-future-trends-the-role-of-ai-in-solo-ai-startup-development). Bold. Probably too bold. But the direction is right.

What's already real: domain experts building software that solves problems they understand deeply. A content creator who [built a $300K ARR SaaS product](https://medium.com/@yumaueno/alex-finn-generating-over-300k-in-annual-revenue-in-2-weeks-with-a-web-app-built-solely-by-ai-e85685c989d8) without writing code. A self-described "hippie who barely knows how to use a microwave" who [built a music visualiser in 24 hours](https://boingboing.net/2025/12/31/non-coder-builds-music-visualizer-in-24-hours-using-ai-assistant.html). A startup founder who called AI his "Series A" because he stopped needing to raise capital.

These aren't developers. They're people who understood a problem deeply and used AI to build the solution. The barrier to creating something -- a tool, a product, a business -- dropped from years of technical training to a conversation with an AI.

I feel this every time I build something new. The vex tool, [Arc](/2025/12/29/building-arc-a-thinking-partner-that-remembers/) -- my personal thinking partner -- the scripts and automations that make my work faster. A year ago, I'd have had an idea and thought "I'd need a developer for that." Now I just build it. The idea-to-working-tool pipeline compressed from weeks to hours.

That's a fundamental shift in who can build things and how much it costs. It doesn't threaten existing businesses. It creates new ones that couldn't have existed before.

## The augmentation expectation

Regardless of whether you're an employee, a business owner, or a solo operator: **Being AI-augmented is heading toward being expected, not optional.**

Maybe not this year. But the trajectory is clear. The accountant who can advise ten clients in the time it used to take for five -- because AI handles the document processing and drafting -- will be the norm, not the exception. The tradie who quotes same-day will be the expectation, not the differentiator. The professional who produces polished, multi-perspective-reviewed work will be baseline, not impressive.

This isn't a threat. It's an upgrade. But only if you're on the right side of it.

I can see it in my own trajectory. The gap between what I could produce a year ago and what I can produce today is enormous. Not because I got smarter. Because the tools I use got dramatically more capable, and I invested the time to learn how to use them properly. That compound effect -- slightly better tools plus slightly better technique, week after week -- adds up fast.

The businesses and professionals who engage with AI now -- who experiment, who learn the frameworks, who figure out where it fits in their specific workflows -- will have a significant head start. Not because AI is a magic wand, but because the compound effect of small time savings, better output quality, and faster response times adds up quickly.

The ones who wait until it's obvious will spend twice as long catching up, because their competitors have been building the muscle memory for years.

## Everyone has an opinion. That's the problem.

The AI conversation is dominated by two camps, and neither is particularly helpful.

The optimists say AI will solve everything, create unlimited abundance, and make work optional. This is fantasy. AI is a tool. A powerful one, but a tool. It hallucinates, it makes mistakes, it produces mediocre output when given mediocre input, and it can't replace the judgement that comes from 20 years in a trade, a profession, or an industry.

The pessimists say AI will destroy jobs, concentrate wealth, and make most people economically irrelevant. This is fear-selling. It ignores every historical precedent of technology creating more work than it destroys, and it underestimates human adaptability.

The useful position is somewhere in between: **AI is going to change the nature of work significantly. Some displacement is inevitable. The best response is proactive engagement -- training, experimentation, and personal research into how these tools apply to your specific situation.**

Not "wait and see." Not "panic and resist." Engage. Try things. Learn what works and what doesn't. Form your own view based on your own experience, not someone else's LinkedIn hot take.

That's what I did. I didn't read about AI and form an opinion. I used it every day for months, pushed it into every corner of my work, found where it was brilliant and where it fell over. My view of AI is based on thousands of hours of actual use, not a TED talk.

## What proactive engagement looks like

This doesn't mean enrolling in an AI degree or hiring a consultant. It means:

**Train.** Find practical, hands-on AI training that's relevant to your industry. Not awareness sessions that tell you AI exists. Not $2,000 corporate programs built for enterprises. Something that gives you frameworks, shows you live demonstrations, and sends you home with specific things to try.

**Experiment.** Pick one task you do repeatedly that drains your time. Draft a quote, summarise a document, respond to a review, write a follow-up email. Try it with AI. See what happens. If the output is rubbish, refine the input. The learning happens in the doing, not the reading.

**Research.** Pay attention to what's happening in your industry specifically. Talk to peers who are using AI. Read case studies from businesses like yours. The generic "AI will change the world" articles are useless. The specific "here's how a plumbing business uses AI to quote same-day" articles are actionable.

**Build the muscle memory.** AI tools reward practice. The first time you use one, you'll get mediocre results. The tenth time, you'll know how to brief it properly. The fiftieth time, it'll feel like a natural extension of how you work. That muscle memory is the competitive advantage -- and it only develops through use.

## The choice

AI is going to change how you work. That's not a prediction -- it's already happening. The question is whether you engage with it on your terms or react to it on someone else's.

The people who will do best aren't the ones with the most technical skill. They're the ones who take the time to understand how AI applies to their specific work, experiment with it deliberately, and build it into their workflows before it becomes mandatory.

I know this because I've lived it. Eighteen years of IT experience gave me a foundation. AI turned that foundation into something I couldn't have built alone. I'm not a better technologist than I was two years ago. But I'm a vastly more capable one -- because the tools I work with amplified everything I already knew.

That opportunity is available to everyone. The tools are accessible. The cost is negligible. The barrier isn't technology.

It's the decision to start.
